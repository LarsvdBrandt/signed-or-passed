# Research dossier: Nike signs Michael Jordan (1984)

Slug: 2026-10-15-nike-signs-michael-jordan | Category: bold-bet | Verdict: SIGNED | Format: long
Researched 2026-10-15. Every source below was opened and read in this run.

## 1. Summary

In the summer and autumn of 1984, Nike was a fast-growing company that had just stumbled. Reebok and Converse were ahead of it in the basketball and aerobics race, it had posted its first quarterly loss, and it had never built a shoe line around one basketball player. Jordan, the third pick of the 1984 NBA draft, had not played a single NBA game. He wore Converse in college and wanted Adidas. Nike (Rob Strasser, Phil Knight, with Sonny Vaccaro pushing the idea) offered him about $500,000 a year for five years in cash (about $2.5M), roughly three times the best known shoe deal of the time. It also offered his own shoe, with input on the design, and royalties. The Air Jordan I went on sale in spring 1985. Nike sold $70M of them by May and more than $100M in the first year (some sources say $126M), against an internal goal of $3M over three years. Jordan Brand is now a $5B+ a year business inside Nike (reported about $7.3B in fiscal 2025). Nike said SIGNED. Important nuance for the script: the "Nike bet its whole basketball budget on one rookie" version comes largely from Vaccaro and the 2023 film "Air". Credit for the deal is disputed. The cash offer was not the biggest on the table, and Nike limited its downside with performance clauses.

## 2. The people

- **Phil Knight**, co-founder and CEO of Nike (b. 1938). Wanted Nike to be "in the entertainment business", not just the shoe business (per Rovell/ESPN, a mid-1970s remark). Signed off on the money. Later said Vaccaro "helped, but he wasn't the MVP"; he named Strasser and Moore.
- **Rob Strasser**, Nike marketing head, former lawyer (1947 to 1993). The executive who decided Nike "needed a basketball icon" (Peter Moore) and who did the deal with agent David Falk. Left Nike in 1987 and went to Adidas. Jordan said he "fell in love with" Strasser's Air Jordan presentation.
- **Sonny Vaccaro**, Nike basketball marketer since 1977. He paid college coaches (about 80) to put teams in Nike. Says he picked Jordan and urged Nike to spend the allotted budget on him. Fired by Nike in 1991. Credit disputed (see myths).
- **Peter Moore**, Nike creative director. Drew the Air Jordan shoe and logo concept. Says "without Sonny there would not be an Air Jordan".
- **David Falk**, Jordan's agent (ProServ). Negotiated with Knight and Strasser. Says Jordan wanted Adidas.
- **George Raveling**, assistant coach of the 1984 US Olympic team, Nike-sponsored. Jordan says Raveling first pushed him to look at Nike.
- **Michael Jordan**, 21, consensus college player of the year (North Carolina), 3rd pick of the 1984 draft. Wanted Adidas.
- **James and Deloris Jordan**, his parents. Convinced him to fly to Nike's HQ.
- **John Fisher** (Spot-Bilt, with O.J. Simpson as promoter) and **Converse** (president John O'Neil, marketing chief Joe Dean): the rival bidders.

## 3. Context in 1984

- Nike: revenue grew from $28.7M (1973) to $867M (end of 1983) (ESPN). Public since 1980 with 7,500 original investors (Nike annual report letter). In fiscal 1984 Nike still made over $40M net income, a 16% return on equity (Knight, 1984 letter quoted in Nike's 2004 report). But in February 1984 it reported its first-ever quarterly loss (ESPN). Its stock hit a low in 1984 and it lagged Reebok and Converse (Biography.com, citing Portland Monthly).
- Nike's image: "They were running guys." Converse was "the basketball shoe company, with Magic, Larry and Dr. J" (Peter Moore). Converse also paid Jordan's coach, Dean Smith, about $10,000 a year to put the brand on North Carolina players (ESPN).
- Reebok: sales $13M in 1983; by mid-decade about $1B, and it overtook Nike as largest US athletic shoe maker before losing the top spot in 1988 (Wikipedia: Reebok).
- Basketball marketing was built around teams and coaches, not individual players (Biography). The top NBA shoe deal known at the time: James Worthy at New Balance, eight years at $150,000 a year (ESPN).
- The 1984 draft (June 19, Madison Square Garden): 1 Olajuwon, 2 Bowie, 3 Jordan, 5 Barkley, 16 Stockton. Widely seen as one of the greatest drafts ever (Wikipedia).
- Adidas was in turmoil: founder Adi Dassler died in 1978, his wife Kathe ran the firm with son Horst and four daughters in separate divisions, and she died later in 1984. Adidas could not move fast (ESPN).

## 4. What the decision-maker KNEW and why SIGNING made sense then

Knew:
- Jordan was the best college player in America and an Olympian (gold in LA, summer 1984). Falk later said "it wasn't like he was some sleeper".
- Jordan had never worn Nike ("I never wore Nike shoes until I signed with Nike") and preferred Adidas and Converse.
- Nike's basketball line was weak. Converse owned the NBA stars.
- Nike was already paying coaches and winning college teams "better than free" (Spot-Bilt's John Fisher).
- A rival, Spot-Bilt, could outbid Nike in pure cash (ESPN).
- He had not played one pro game. Nobody knew if he would be a star. (Falk disputes the "unknown" framing, but the pro question was real.)

Why it made sense:
- Nike could not beat Converse by copying it. Converse offered about $100,000 a year and "treat you like all our other superstars". Nike could offer the one thing Converse could not: be the only star, with a signature shoe and input on design.
- The downside was small relative to the company. $500K a year was about 0.06% of Nike's $867M sales (our calculation).
- The downside was also limited by the contract: if Jordan did not win Rookie of the Year, make an All-Star team or average 20 points a game in his first three years, Nike could end the deal two years early (Falk via ESPN).
- Knight's philosophy: the promotional value of a star is worth more than the shoe revenue (Fisher paraphrasing Knight). Nike was already losing ground, so doing the safe thing was also a risk.

## 5. The decision moment

- Who/where: Summer to autumn 1984. After the Olympics Jordan told Falk "I have no interest in going there. Just do what you need to do to get me with adidas" (Falk via ESPN). Falk called Jordan's parents. The family flew to Nike HQ in Beaverton, Oregon (two buildings at the time).
- In the room: Strasser, designer Peter Moore, Howard White, Sonny Vaccaro. Jordan watched a highlight tape set to the Pointer Sisters' "Jump", saw a red-and-black shoe design, and heard Nike would shape the shoe to his taste. Phil Knight walked in during the second room. Strasser pulled out two die-cast Mercedes cars as a nod to Jordan's love of cars (Falk's anecdote, ESPN).
- On the table: $500,000 a year in cash for five years (about $2.5M), plus stock options (Falk: about $7M in total over five years if Nike kept the deal), royalties on Air Jordan products (rate never publicly confirmed), his own shoe line. Falk also says he made Nike commit $1M to marketing in the first six months (ESPN).
- Rivals: Converse about $100,000 a year. Adidas: "almost no interest" (ESPN) or $100,000 (Biography); the film shows Adidas matching Nike (dramatic licence). Spot-Bilt: the highest cash bid (ESPN). Jordan took Nike's numbers back to Adidas ("if you come anywhere close, I'll sign with you guys"), and Adidas did not.
- Final numbers were reviewed at the L'Ermitage hotel in Beverly Hills with Strasser, Falk and Vaccaro in the room (Vaccaro, via ESPN).
- Signed: late October 1984. Wikipedia gives October 26, 1984. Not confirmed by a second opened source.

## 6. What happened next (timeline)

- 1964 Blue Ribbon Sports founded. 1971 renamed Nike. 1977 Vaccaro starts at Nike. 1980 Nike goes public.
- Feb 1984: first quarterly loss.
- Summer 1984: LA Olympics. June 19: the draft.
- Late Oct 1984: Jordan signs. First Air Jordan produced Nov 17, 1984 (Wikipedia). Oct 18, 1984: the NBA's executive VP Russ Granik writes that red-and-black Nike shoes are prohibited. Nike pays the reported $5,000-per-game fines and runs the "banned" ad.
- 1984-85 season: Jordan plays every game, wins Rookie of the Year.
- March/April 1985: Air Jordan I in stores at $65. $70M sold by May. More than $100M by year end (Wikipedia: $126M, flagged by the article as disputed).
- 1986: Reebok overtakes Nike in the US (Wikipedia: Reebok, "mid-decade").
- 1987: Strasser leaves Nike. 1991: Vaccaro fired by Nike.
- 1997: Jordan Brand launched as its own brand.
- 2022: Jordan Brand revenue $5.1B. About $256M of royalties to Jordan (Biography; Wikipedia gives $150 to 256M).
- Fiscal 2025 (to May 2025): Jordan Brand revenue about $7.3B, down 16% (Sportico).

## 7. Consequences in numbers

- Deal: about $2.5M over five years in cash (Wikipedia says six years; see claims).
- Year one: Air Jordan sales $70M by May 1985, more than $100M by year end, vs goal of $3M in three years. Conservative: "more than $100 million". Range: $100M to $126M.
- Today: Jordan Brand $5.1B (2022), about $7.3B (fiscal 2025). Nike total revenue was $46.4B in fiscal 2026 (Nike press release), about 50 times its $867M in 1983.
- Ratio: $7.3B a year vs a $2.5M contract is roughly 2,900 times (our calculation). Say "thousands of times".
- For Jordan: a reported $150 to 256M in 2022 alone. Estimated about $300M in the latest year, mostly Nike royalties (Sportico estimate).
- Reebok passed Nike in the mid-80s, but lost the top spot in 1988 (Wikipedia: Reebok).

## 9. Myths and the truth

1. **Myth: Jordan wanted Nike.** Truth: he wore Converse in college, preferred Adidas, and almost did not fly to Oregon. His parents pushed him.
2. **Myth: Nike put its whole $250,000 basketball budget on Jordan, with Vaccaro risking his job.** Truth: the $250K figure appears in the 2023 film and in articles that repeat it. We found no primary source. The real cash offer was $500K a year. That "all the allotted budget on one player" claim is Vaccaro's account, and Knight disputes his role. Say "Vaccaro says...".
3. **Myth: Vaccaro single-handedly landed Jordan.** Truth: Knight credits Strasser and Moore, Jordan credits Raveling and Strasser, Moore says Vaccaro and Strasser, Falk says he did the deal with Knight and Strasser. The credit is disputed.
4. **Myth: Jordan's mother invented the royalty.** Truth: that is a film scene. We have no confirmed source for who asked for the royalty. The rate has never been made public. Ignore the "25%" and "5%" numbers circulating online.
5. **Myth: Nike beat Adidas with a matching offer plus a Mercedes in a bidding war.** Truth: in Falk's account Adidas showed almost no interest. The Mercedes was a die-cast toy car in the pitch. Spot-Bilt made the highest cash bid.
6. **Myth: first-year sales were $162M.** That is the film's epilogue. Documented numbers: more than $100M (ESPN) or $126M (Wikipedia, Biography).
7. **Myth: the banned shoe was the Air Jordan I.** Slate (citing the Chicago Tribune) says the first shoe he wore in games was a red-and-black Air Ship mocked up to look like the Air Jordan. The NBA letter was dated October 18, 1984. Safe wording: "the NBA banned his red and black Nike shoes."
8. **Myth: nobody knew who Jordan was.** Truth: he was national college player of the year. The risk was how he would do as a pro and whether a signature line would sell.

## 10. Three candidate lessons for entrepreneurs

1. **When you are behind, copying the leader is the real risk.** Nike could not out-Converse Converse, so it offered what the leader could not: a signature shoe for one star.
2. **Concentrate the bet, cap the downside.** Put real weight behind the one thing you believe in, but write in the exits (the performance clauses) so a miss is survivable.
3. **Share the upside.** Royalties turned Jordan from a paid face into a partner. The same deal that cost $500K a year made him, and Nike, rich.

(Also a "bonus" lesson: success has a thousand fathers. Write down who decided what.)
