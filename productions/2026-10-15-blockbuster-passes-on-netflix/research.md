# Research dossier: Blockbuster passes on buying Netflix (PASSED)

Slug: 2026-10-15-blockbuster-passes-on-netflix · Format: long · Prepared 2026-10-15

## 1. Summary

In 2000, at Blockbuster's Dallas headquarters, Netflix co-founders Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph (with CFO Barry McCarthy) put a $50 million price on Netflix. That was a DVD-by-mail start-up with about 300,000 subscribers and a 2000 net loss of $57.4 million. Blockbuster, the dominant US video chain with roughly $5 billion in yearly revenue, said no. This is the first and best-known "PASSED" story. It is also half-myth. All sides agree on the meeting and the $50M number. They disagree on whether it was a serious sale offer, whether CEO John Antioco was in the room, and whether anyone "laughed". The decision looks reasonable for 2000: dot-com crash, a money-losing target, and a core business that was still growing. The real fight came later. Blockbuster launched its own online service (2004) and Total Access (late 2006), then lost its way in a clash between Antioco and an activist-influenced board. Antioco left in 2007. Blockbuster filed for Chapter 11 in September 2010. Netflix is today worth hundreds of billions of dollars (see c43 for the range).

## 3. Context in 2000

- **Blockbuster.** Founded 1985 by David Cook in Dallas (s2, s8). Owned by Viacom 1994–2004 (s2). Peak was 2004 with 9,094 stores and 84,300 staff (s2). In 2000, revenue was about $4.96B, with 6,254 company-operated stores and about 7,700 stores overall including franchises (c22, c23: *unverified*, taken from a search summary of the FY2001 10-K). Late fees were a huge profit source, about $800M/yr at peak (s8, unverified; Blockbuster's own FY2004 10-K projected $400–450M of "extended viewing fee" revenue for 2005, per s8).
- **Netflix.** Founded 29 Aug 1997 (s1). Started as rent-or-buy DVD by mail. Monthly subscription from Sept 1999 (s1, s4). In 2000: revenue $35.9M, operating loss $57.6M, net loss $57.4M (s4). About 292,000 subscribers at end of 2000 (s7, quoting the S-1; s8 says about 300,000).
- **Technology.** DVD was brand new: the fastest-selling consumer electronics device in history (s4). About 25M US households had a DVD player at end of 2001, up 91% that year (s4, citing Adams Media). The 1997 home-video market Hastings and Randolph targeted was $16B (s1). In 2001, consumers spent $21B on video rentals and purchases (s4).
- **Money and mood.** The dot-com bubble was bursting. Money-losing internet companies were being doubted (s1: Netflix "was suffering losses" during the bubble). Netflix itself cut a third of its staff after 9/11 and delayed its IPO (s1).

## 4. What the decision-maker knew, and why "no" made sense then

(Fairness section. Use in the script.)

1. Netflix lost more in 2000 ($57.4M net) than the $50M it was asking (s4). A buyer would pay for a business that had burned more than the price in one year.
2. Antioco says Netflix itself did not seem to believe it was worth $50M (s7, quoting his LinkedIn).
3. Netflix had about 300k subscribers. Blockbuster had tens of millions of members and thousands of stores (c22 and c23, to be confirmed; s13 gives HQ, the 10-K body is not confirmed).
4. The core business was healthy. In 2000, revenue was up about 11% (c22, unverified).
5. Dot-com valuations were collapsing. Netflix was exactly the kind of company investors were doubting.
6. The proposal, as Hastings recalls it (via s7), was not "buy us and shut us". It was "let us run your online arm". For a retail CEO, that was a partnership pitch, not a bargain.
7. Antioco's own account: no serious acquisition talk happened and he only greeted the guests (s7 quoting s9).
8. Blockbuster was majority-controlled by Viacom in 2000 (s2: Viacom was the parent 1994–2004). Whether Antioco could have done a deal alone is untested (unverified; flag for the script as a question, not a fact).
9. A $50M price was about 1% of Blockbuster's yearly revenue, so it was affordable. The risk was not the price. It was the bet on an unproven model. This is the honest tension for the "Would you sign?" freeze.

## 5. The decision moment

- **Where:** Blockbuster's headquarters in Dallas, Texas. Everyone agrees on the Dallas location (s7 quoting Variety 2025, "everyone agrees there was a meeting", and s8). Blockbuster's address in its SEC filings was 1201 Elm Street, Dallas (s13 cover page, FY2001 10-K; also s5, 2007). s8 says "Renner Road", which conflicts with the filings. Do not use it.
- **When:** *Disputed.* Wikipedia and s8 say September 2000. Hastings places it "early in 2000" (per s7). Hastings's own details (about 300,000 subscribers, $57M annual loss) fit late 2000, not early 2000. Safest wording: "in 2000".
- **Who:** Hastings, Randolph, McCarthy for Netflix (Randolph's account, s7, s8). For Blockbuster, Antioco and executives. Antioco says he was not part of the meeting and only stopped by to greet the visitors (s7 quoting s9).
- **On the table:** Netflix named $50M (s1, s7, s8). All three people involved mention $50M, so the number is solid. The structure is disputed. Randolph and Wikipedia say they offered to sell the company. Hastings (via s7) says Netflix would run Blockbuster's online business. Antioco says they "apparently offered to sell their company for $50 million" (s7 quoting s9).
- **Outcome:** Blockbuster declined. In the book version, Antioco said the "dot-com hysteria is completely overblown" (s1; Randolph's recollection, see section 8).
- **No document.** No contemporary term sheet, offer letter or minutes has been published. Everything is memory, 19 to 26 years later.

**Total Access.** Blockbuster Online, then Total Access, was a serious attempt. At the end of 2006 it had about 2.2M subscribers (about 2M paying), up 700k in Q4 alone, and management expected 3M by end of Q1 2007 (s6). Antioco says it triggered serious talks between the two firms in early 2007 (s7 quoting s10; his claim only, uncorroborated). It was costly. It cost "some investment in the first half" (s6), and the board fought it. Hard fact: in 2006 Blockbuster's stores still produced the money (2006 operating income $79.1M, free cash flow $250.9M, s6).

## 7. Consequences in numbers

- Netflix 2025: revenue $45.2B, operating income $13.3B, net income $11.0B, ~16,000 employees (s1, citing the 10-K).
- Netflix market value: *disputed*. Wikipedia says an estimate of $450B in 2026 (s1). A search summary of market-data sites says ~$293B in mid-October 2026 (not opened). Use "hundreds of billions".
- 2010s: Netflix was the best S&P 500 stock, +3,693% total return (s1).
- $50M against 2025 revenue: 2025 revenue is about 900 times the asking price (computed from c42).
- Netflix at IPO (May 2002): 20,648,074 shares after the offering at $15 gives about $310M implied value, six times the "ask" 20 months earlier (computed from s4). The IPO alone raised $82.5M gross (s4).
- Blockbuster 2010: revenue $3.24B, operating loss $78.8M, net loss $268M, 25,000 employees, down from 84,300 in 2004 (s2; unverified single source).
- Bankruptcy filing: assets $1.02B vs debt $1.46B (s8; unverified).
- Blockbuster brand sold to Dish in 2011 (~1,700 stores, s2; ~$320M, s8 only).
- Aftermath oddity: the shell company BB Liquidating Inc. gained notoriety in the 2021 GameStop short squeeze (s2).
- Antioco's own exit: lump sum $4.9875M instead of the $13.5M he would have got under the old contract (s5).

## 9. Myths and the truth

| Myth | Truth |
|---|---|
| Blockbuster "laughed" Netflix out of the room | Only Randolph's account (and his reading of a "tiny, involuntary" smile). Hastings (via s7) calls it "curiosity". Antioco says he was not in the meeting. Unproven. |
| Antioco personally turned down a serious $50M offer | Antioco says there were no serious talks and he only greeted the guests (s7/s9). Randolph says he was at the table. Disputed. |
| Netflix was clearly worth far more than $50M in 2000 | It lost $57.4M that year with ~300k subscribers (s4). The success was far from obvious. |
| Blockbuster ignored online rental | False. It built Blockbuster Online (2004) and Total Access (2006), reaching ~2.2M online subscribers by end of 2006 (s6). |
| The Dallas "no" killed Blockbuster | Weak. Blockbuster went bankrupt in 2010 after a debt-laden split from Viacom (unverified here), a board fight, store leases, Redbox and Netflix (s8, s2). Some analysts argue the bigger mistake came in 2007 (s7), when the online strategy was abandoned. |
| Netflix alone killed Blockbuster | s2 names poor leadership, the Great Recession, Netflix mail, video on demand and Redbox kiosks. |
| "Blockbuster could have bought Netflix for $50M and avoided bankruptcy" | $50M is about 1% of its 2000 revenue (computed; unverified base). Whether owning Netflix would have saved it is a counterfactual. |
| s8's claim that Total Access launched in Nov 2004 | Wrong. Blockbuster's own Feb 2007 release says it launched about five months earlier (s6). |

## 10. Candidate lessons for entrepreneurs

1. **Judge the decision by what was knowable.** In 2000 the no was defensible. The lesson is not "never say no to a start-up" but "know what you are buying: a company or an option on the future?" For a small price, an option on the thing that could kill your core business is cheap insurance.
2. **The famous mistake is rarely the fatal one.** Blockbuster did try online and was starting to win (2.2M subscribers). The strategy was then cut under board pressure. Commit to a transition, fund it, and align your board before you start it.
3. **Fixed costs turn a slow disruption into a death sentence.** Blockbuster carried thousands of leases and large late-fee profits. Every move to the new model cut the old model's cash cow (late fees, store traffic). Plan how to pay for the bridge years.
