# Script: Blockbuster passes on Netflix

Slug: 2026-10-15-blockbuster-passes-on-netflix · Format: long · Verdict: PASSED · Voice: Kokoro bm_george, speed 1.15 · Remake v2 (Retention Playbook, after Lars's pilot feedback)

## 1. HOOK (0:00-0:05)

**Fifty million dollars.** That was the price tag on Netflix. And **Blockbuster** said no.

> claims: c15, c13

## 2. PROMISE (0:05-0:21)

Today, Netflix is worth **hundreds of billions**. Blockbuster? Down to **one store**. In Oregon. Dumbest no in business history? Not quite. By the end, you'll see why saying no made sense. And the **boardroom fight**, seven years later, that nobody remembers.

> claims: c43, c41, c14, c34, c35

## 3. SET THE SCENE

Dallas, Texas. **Blockbuster** headquarters. The year is two thousand.

> claims: c13, c14

Blockbuster was born right here in Dallas, in nineteen eighty-five. Now it rules home video. **Thousands of stores.** Close to **five billion dollars** a year in revenue. And still growing, up about eleven percent.

> claims: c20, c23, c22

The boss is **John Antioco**. A retail lifer. Around twenty years at Seven-Eleven. Then Circle K. Then Taco Bell. In nineteen ninety-seven, he took the top job at Blockbuster. Antioco knows how to make **stores** pay.

> claims: c26

Across the table: three visitors from California. **Reed Hastings** and **Marc Randolph**, who founded Netflix in nineteen ninety-seven. And their finance chief, Barry McCarthy.

> claims: c13, c1

Their idea? A video rental shop with **no shop**. DVDs by mail. You pick films on a website. The discs land in your mailbox. Since September nineteen ninety-nine, one flat **monthly fee**.

> claims: c4

And DVD is spreading fast. By the end of the next year, about **twenty-five million** American homes will own a player. Nearly double. That year, Americans will spend **twenty-one billion dollars** renting and buying videos.

> claims: c49

Now look at Netflix's own numbers for two thousand. About **three hundred thousand** subscribers. Revenue: thirty-five point nine million dollars. And the loss: **fifty-seven point four million**. Netflix spends far more than it earns.

> claims: c7, c5, c6

Remember that number. Because then the Netflix team names its price. **Fifty million dollars.**

> claims: c15, c6

Every version of this story agrees on that number. Almost everything else is **disputed**. Who was in the room. What was said. Even the month. Hold on to that.

> claims: c15, c14, c16, c17

## 4. THE PRESSURE

So you're in **Antioco's chair**. First, the case for saying no.

> claims: (none; framing)

Reason one: **the math**. Netflix is on its way to losing more this year than its own price tag. And buying a company means buying its losses. Next year too. And the year after. Nobody can promise when it will turn a profit.

> claims: c50, c6

Reason two: **the mood**. In two thousand, the **dot-com bubble** peaked and began to burst. Internet companies that lost money were suddenly suspect. Netflix was exactly that kind of company.

> claims: c54, c6

Reason three: **scale**. About three hundred thousand subscribers against thousands of stores. Why bet on a tiny website when your own shops are still growing?

> claims: c7, c23, c22

Reason four: who really decides? Blockbuster's parent company is the media giant **Viacom**. Could Antioco buy Netflix on his own? We simply don't know.

> claims: c24

Sounds like an easy no. But here's where it gets **interesting**.

> claims: (none; re-hook)

Fifty million was about **one percent** of Blockbuster's yearly revenue. Small change for a giant. The risk wasn't the price. It was the idea. A rental business with **no stores**. No shelves. No counters. No rent.

> claims: c51, c22, c15

And if renting by mail caught on? Customers might stop walking into Blockbuster at all. For one percent of a year's revenue, it could own the thing. The thing that might **replace it**. Cheap insurance? Or an expensive distraction?

> claims: c49, c51

## 5. FREEZE

So here it is. A start-up losing more than its own price tag. Your own business, still growing. **Fifty million dollars** on the table. Would you sign? Comment **SIGN** or **PASS** before I tell you.

[PAUSE 3s]

> claims: c50, c22, c15

## 6. REVEAL

[PAUSE 1s]

Blockbuster **passed**. No deal.

> claims: c13, c15

But how that no happened depends on who you ask. And this is where it gets **strange**.

> claims: c16, c17, c19

**Marc Randolph** tells the famous version. In his twenty nineteen book, *That Will Never Work*, he recalls Antioco struggling not to laugh. In twenty twenty-three, he wrote on Twitter that Blockbuster "**laughed us out of the room**." That's how Randolph remembers it.

> claims: c17, c18

**John Antioco** remembers it very differently. In twenty twenty-one, he wrote on LinkedIn: "I can say with complete confidence that there were never any serious acquisition talks in 2000." He says he wasn't even in the meeting. He only stopped by to **greet the guests**.

> claims: c16

And **Reed Hastings**? He has reportedly said he doesn't think it was serious. More **curiosity** than anything else.

> claims: c19

Three men. **Three memories.** Told nearly twenty years later, or more. Only three things are certain. A meeting in Dallas. A number: fifty million. And no deal.

> claims: c16, c17, c18, c19, c13, c15

## 7. AFTERMATH

But that's not the crazy part. Watch what happens **next**.

> claims: (none; re-hook)

Netflix keeps going. In May two thousand and two, it **goes public**. Its shares hit the stock market at fifteen dollars each. The offering alone raises eighty-two and a half million dollars. More than the Dallas price. At that price, the whole company is valued at about **three hundred and ten million dollars**. Six times its Dallas price tag.

> claims: c10, c15

By then, it has more than **six hundred thousand** subscribers. About double what it had in two thousand. More than eleven and a half thousand titles. Nineteen ninety-five a month.

> claims: c9, c7

And Blockbuster? Here's the part almost everyone skips. Blockbuster **fights back**.

> claims: c27, c29

In two thousand and four, it hits its peak. **Nine thousand and ninety-four stores.** And eighty-four thousand three hundred employees. That same year, it splits from Viacom. And launches its own online service: **Blockbuster Online**.

> claims: c21, c24, c27

January two thousand and five. It drops most **late fees**, the fines for bringing a movie back late.

> claims: c28

Then a new player arrives. The activist investor **Carl Icahn**. That's an investor who buys in to shake things up. He builds a stake of nearly **ten percent**. In May two thousand and five, he wins a seat on the board.

> claims: c33

And the numbers turn ugly. For two thousand and five: a net loss of **five hundred and eighty-eight million dollars**. More than half is a write-down. An admission that some assets were worth less than the books said.

> claims: c32

Then, in autumn two thousand and six, Blockbuster plays its best card. **Total Access.**

> claims: c29

Rent online. Return the disc to a Blockbuster store. Swap it for a **free rental**. Netflix has no stores. It can't match that.

> claims: c29

And it works. By the end of two thousand and six: about **two point two million** online subscribers. Seven hundred thousand added in three months alone. The target: three million by the end of March. And Blockbuster is **back in profit**. Fifty-four point seven million dollars.

> claims: c30, c31

Antioco says Netflix came **back to the table**. Serious talks, he says, in early two thousand and seven. That's his account.

> claims: c52

So Blockbuster has a shot? Not so fast. Here's the **boardroom fight** I promised you.

> claims: (none; re-hook, pays off the promise)

January two thousand and seven. The board offers Antioco a bonus of two point two eight million dollars. He says he's owed **seven point six five million**. In March, they settle. His lump-sum exit payment: just under five million. His old contract would have paid **thirteen and a half million**.

> claims: c34

That summer, **Jim Keyes** takes over as CEO. The man who launched Total Access is **gone**.

> claims: c35, c29

That same year, Netflix starts **streaming**. Films over the internet. No disc at all.

> claims: c45

Now the fall. Twenty-third of September, two thousand and ten. Blockbuster files for **Chapter Eleven**, American bankruptcy protection. In two thousand and eleven, Dish Network buys it out of bankruptcy. About seventeen hundred stores. In early two thousand and fourteen, the last company-owned stores **close**.

> claims: c36, c39, c40

Today, one Blockbuster is still open. A single franchised store. In **Bend, Oregon**.

> claims: c41

And Netflix? In twenty twenty-five, revenue of **forty-five point two billion dollars**. Profit: eleven billion dollars. About **nine hundred times** that fifty million price tag. And a company worth hundreds of billions.

> claims: c42, c51, c43

So did one no in Dallas kill Blockbuster? Probably not on its own. Blockbuster did fight for the **online future**. For a while, it was gaining ground. Would owning Netflix have saved it? Nobody can know.

> claims: c30, c29

## 8. VERDICT

Fifty million dollars, on the table in Dallas. Blockbuster **PASSED**.

> claims: c13, c15

## 9. LESSON

When a small company could replace your core business, don't just ask what it's worth today. Ask what it **costs you if it wins**.

Next time: another giant, another deal on the table. Would you sign that one?
