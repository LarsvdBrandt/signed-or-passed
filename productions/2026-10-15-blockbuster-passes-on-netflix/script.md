# Script: Blockbuster passes on Netflix

Slug: 2026-10-15-blockbuster-passes-on-netflix · Format: long · Verdict: PASSED · Voice: Kokoro bm_george, speed 1.1 · Revision 1 (after fact check)

## 1. COLD OPEN (0:00-0:20)

Fifty million dollars. That was the price on a small, money-losing start-up that mailed DVDs to people's homes. The company it was pitched to had thousands of stores. Today, that start-up is Netflix, worth hundreds of billions of dollars. The company with the stores was Blockbuster. It is down to one last store, in Oregon. So, sitting in that chair, would you have bought it?

> claims: c15, c6, c23, c43, c41

## 2. SET THE SCENE

Dallas, Texas. The year two thousand. This is where Blockbuster was born, back in nineteen eighty-five, and by now it is everywhere. Thousands of stores. Close to five billion dollars a year in revenue. And it is not even fully its own boss: its parent company is the media giant Viacom.

> claims: c13, c14, c20, c22, c23, c24

The man in charge is John Antioco. A retail lifer. He spent around twenty years at the convenience chain Seven-Eleven, then ran Circle K and later Taco Bell, before he took the top job at Blockbuster in nineteen ninety-seven. Antioco knows stores. He knows how to make them pay.

> claims: c26

Now picture a meeting room at Blockbuster's headquarters. On one side, Blockbuster's executives. On the other, three visitors from California. Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph founded Netflix in nineteen ninety-seven, in a small town called Scotts Valley. With them is their finance chief, Barry McCarthy.

> claims: c1, c13

Netflix rents DVDs by mail. Since September nineteen ninety-nine, customers pay a flat monthly subscription. You pick your films on a website, and the discs arrive in your mailbox. And DVD is spreading fast. By the end of the following year, about twenty-five million American households will own a DVD player, nearly double the year before. That year, Americans will spend twenty-one billion dollars renting and buying videos. A huge market. And Blockbuster stands right at its front door.

> claims: c4, c49, c23

Now look at Netflix's numbers for two thousand. About three hundred thousand subscribers. Revenue for the year: thirty-five point nine million dollars. And a net loss of fifty-seven point four million. A net loss means that after every bill is paid, the company is that much in the red. Netflix is spending far more than it earns.

> claims: c5, c6, c7

Then the Netflix team names its price. Fifty million dollars. Everyone who has told this story agrees on that number. Almost everything else about this meeting is disputed. Even the month. Hold that thought.

> claims: c15, c14

## 3. THE PRESSURE

So put yourself in John Antioco's shoes. Why would you say no?

> claims: (none)

First, the math. Netflix lost more money in that one year than the price it was asking. You would be paying fifty million dollars for a business on its way to losing more than fifty million dollars that year.

> claims: c50, c6, c15

And buying a company means buying its losses, too. Next year, and the year after that. Nobody could promise when Netflix would ever turn a profit.

> claims: c6

Second, the mood. In two thousand, the dot-com bubble peaked and began to burst. Internet companies that lost money were suddenly being doubted, and Netflix was exactly that kind of company.

> claims: c54, c6

Third, the scale. Netflix had about three hundred thousand subscribers. Blockbuster had thousands of stores, and its revenue was still growing, up about eleven percent on the year before. Why bet on a tiny website when your own shops are doing just fine?

> claims: c7, c22, c23

And fourth, who really decides? Blockbuster answered to Viacom. Whether Antioco could have bought Netflix on his own, we simply don't know.

> claims: c24

But there is a case for yes, too. Fifty million dollars was about one percent of Blockbuster's yearly revenue. It could afford it. The risk wasn't the price. The risk was the idea: a rental business with no stores at all. No shelves. No counters. No rent.

> claims: c51, c22, c15

And DVD was spreading fast. If renting by mail caught on, customers might one day stop walking into Blockbuster at all. For about one percent of a year's revenue, Blockbuster could own the very thing that might replace it. Cheap insurance? Or an expensive distraction?

> claims: c49, c51

## 4. FREEZE

So here you are. Dallas, two thousand. A start-up losing more in a year than its own price tag. Your own business, still growing. Fifty million dollars on the table. Would you sign?

[PAUSE 3s]

> claims: c13, c14, c15, c50, c22

## 5. REVEAL

Blockbuster said no. There was no deal.

> claims: c13, c15

But how that no was delivered depends on who you ask. Marc Randolph tells the famous version. In his twenty nineteen book, That Will Never Work, he recalls Antioco struggling not to laugh. In twenty twenty-three, he wrote on Twitter that Blockbuster "laughed us out of the room." Keep in mind: that is how Randolph remembers it.

> claims: c17, c18

John Antioco remembers it very differently. In twenty twenty-one, he wrote on LinkedIn: "I can say with complete confidence that there were never any serious acquisition talks in 2000." He says he wasn't even part of the meeting. He only stopped by to greet the guests.

> claims: c16

And Reed Hastings? He has reportedly said he doesn't think it was serious. More curiosity than anything else.

> claims: c19

So one of the most famous rejections in business rests on three memories, told nearly two decades later or more. What everyone agrees on is simple. There was a meeting in Dallas. There was a number: fifty million dollars. And Blockbuster did not buy Netflix.

> claims: c13, c15, c16, c17, c18, c19

## 6. AFTERMATH

Netflix kept going. In May two thousand and two, it went public. That means it sold shares on the stock market for the first time. Fifteen dollars a share. Eighty-two and a half million dollars raised. At that price, the whole company was valued at roughly three hundred and ten million dollars. About six times the number it had named in Dallas. By then, Netflix had more than six hundred thousand subscribers, about double what it had in two thousand, and more than eleven and a half thousand titles. The standard plan cost nineteen dollars and ninety-five cents a month.

> claims: c10, c9, c7, c15

And Blockbuster? This is the part of the story people usually skip. Blockbuster did not sit still. In two thousand and four, it hit its peak: nine thousand and ninety-four stores. That same year it split from Viacom and launched its own online rental service, Blockbuster Online. In January two thousand and five, it dropped most late fees, the fines for bringing a movie back late.

> claims: c21, c24, c27, c28

The numbers swung hard. In two thousand and five, Blockbuster reported a net loss of five hundred and eighty-eight million dollars. More than half of that was a write-down, an accounting admission that some assets were worth less than the books said. By then, the activist investor Carl Icahn had built a stake of nearly ten percent, and in May two thousand and five he won a seat on the board. An activist investor buys a big slice of a company to push it in a new direction.

> claims: c32, c33

Then, in the autumn of two thousand and six, came Total Access. Rent your discs online, then return them to a Blockbuster store and swap them for free. Netflix had no stores to match that. And it worked. By the end of two thousand and six, Blockbuster's online service had about two point two million subscribers, seven hundred thousand of them added in the last three months of the year alone. The company was back in profit, too: fifty-four point seven million dollars for the year.

> claims: c29, c30, c31

Antioco says Total Access brought Netflix back to the table, and that serious talks between the two companies began in early two thousand and seven. That is his account.

> claims: c52

But inside Blockbuster, a different fight had begun. In January two thousand and seven, the board offered Antioco a bonus of two point two eight million dollars. He said he was owed seven point six five million. In March, they settled. His lump-sum exit payment was just under five million dollars, instead of the thirteen and a half million his old contract would have paid. Antioco left, and Jim Keyes took over as CEO that same year. The man who had launched Total Access was gone.

> claims: c34, c35, c29

Also in two thousand and seven, Netflix started streaming. Films over the internet. No disc at all.

> claims: c45

On the twenty-third of September, two thousand and ten, Blockbuster filed for Chapter Eleven. That is American bankruptcy protection: a court holds off the lenders while a company tries to fix its debts. In two thousand and eleven, Dish Network bought Blockbuster out of bankruptcy, with about one thousand seven hundred stores. The last company-owned stores closed in early two thousand and fourteen. Today, one franchised Blockbuster, run by a local owner under the famous name, is still open. In Bend, Oregon.

> claims: c36, c39, c40, c41

And Netflix? In two thousand and twenty-five, it brought in forty-five point two billion dollars in revenue. That is about nine hundred times the fifty million it once named in Dallas. And the company is worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

> claims: c42, c51, c43

So was that one no in Dallas what killed Blockbuster? Probably not on its own. Blockbuster did fight for the online future, and for a while it was gaining ground. Whether owning Netflix back in two thousand would have saved it, nobody can know.

> claims: c30, c51

## 7. VERDICT

Fifty million dollars, on the table in Dallas. Blockbuster passed.

> claims: c13, c15

## 8. LESSON

When a small company could one day replace your core business, don't only ask what it's worth today; ask what it would cost you if it wins.

So, would you have signed? Tell me in the comments.

> claims: (none; interpretation, no new facts)
